Walk past any broker in Ulaanbaatar and you'll see the same pitch: buy US stocks from Mongolia. TDB Securities, Golomt Capital, MICC, Ard, Apex, Mandal, Goodsec, BDSec. Most of them route through Interactive Brokers, Saxo, Bank of China or Bloomberg. Mongolia is already on IBKR's country list.

So access is solved. That's the starting point, not the finding.

The question I wanted to answer is different: **which money can actually move, and what product would make a brokerage bigger, not just busier?**

My short answer: a firm doesn't grow by adding one more execution badge. It grows when a slice of household savings moves into something that survives three things at once: the tugrik deposit rate, the tax wrapper, and the fact that the biggest official pools are either closed or already invested.

## The real competitor is the deposit

The numbers that matter:

| Figure | Value |
|---|---|
| Household tugrik deposits, end-Aug 2026 | **₮23.7 tn** |
| New tugrik deposit rate, Aug 2026 | **11.7%** |
| Future Heritage Fund on USD deposits, 2025 | **4.16%** |
| Tugrik vs USD, year to Jan 2026 | **~3.8% weaker** |

Put dollar cash plus a modest depreciation next to a tugrik deposit, and the deposit still wins. In a calm year for the tugrik, a foreign product loses a straight yield comparison.

So stop selling it as "a better return." That's an argument the deposit wins. A foreign sleeve is **insurance**: against one currency and one banking system. Sell it as that.

(The trade-weighted picture is worse than the dollar cross, by the way. The NEER fell about 9.8% because of the yuan, the ruble and the euro. That's the case for insurance, not for yield.)

## Three pools of money, one real prospect

**Household deposits: open, but hard.** ₮23.7 trillion, 82% of all tugrik deposits. Nothing in the currency rules stops a resident buying foreign securities through a bank. What stops them is the 11.7% deposit, the FX trip, and the dollar cash and gold people already keep outside any broker.

**Social insurance: closed.** Its free balance can only go into government securities, Bank of Mongolia paper, the treasury account and deposits at systemic banks. In 2026 the government capped long-term government and central-bank paper at ₮300 billion. This pool is not buying a foreign ETF.

**Future Heritage Fund: already abroad.** At end-2025, USD 1.336 billion, 83% of its assets, sat in short deposits at foreign central banks, placed through the Bank of Mongolia. Return 4.16%. Its policy also allows foreign bonds and big equity indexes. It didn't start there.

That last point is the useful one. Private clients will never hand a broker the sovereign fund, so a pitch to "manage the SWF" is not a business plan. But you can **copy the fund's order of risk**: USD cash-like first, an equity index only after that.

## A US stock ticket doesn't match the invoice

Mongolia exported USD 15.7 billion in 2025. **89.4% went to China.** Switzerland took 6.9%, almost all of it gold. Coal was 40.9% of exports and copper 41.4%.

That means two different clients, and two different products:

- **Households** already think in dollars. The honest product is short USD government paper. It competes with a dollar bill and with the deposit, not with a stock tip.
- **Exporters** have yuan exposure whether they invoice in dollars or not, because the buyer is in China. A sleeve tied to the yuan, copper and coal cash flows, and gold matches their revenue.

US single names are a third product, for the client who asks. They're not the franchise.

## The wrapper is the product

A foreign fund can't be publicly offered in Mongolia, and private placement needs approval. So the choice is really about the shell:

| Shell | What the client holds | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Weak default** | A US-listed ETF in their own name | US dividends to a non-treaty country are generally withheld at 30%. US shares count for US estate tax above USD 60,000, at rates up to 40%. Mongolia isn't on the treaty lists. |
| **Better shell** | An Ireland-domiciled UCITS fund | US tax sits inside the fund, often 15% under the US–Ireland treaty. The share is Irish, so the estate-tax picture changes. |
| **Scale vehicle** | A Mongolian fund that holds the UCITS or T-bills | Heavier: a manager licence, custody, a prospectus. It's also the only version with a name, a monthly debit and a recurring fee. |

Mongolia still taxes dividends, interest and gains at 10% on top, with a foreign tax credit only under conditions. Any factsheet yield that ignores withholding, estate tax and that 10% will get taken apart by the client's lawyer. This is the shape of the question, not a tax opinion. Get counsel before anything is printed.

## The fee isn't the commission

A rough scale, not a forecast:

**₮23.7 tn × 1% that moves × 0.50% a year ≈ ₮1.2 bn gross per year.**

If you hate my 1%, use your own number. The point is the unit of revenue. The commission on a US share is the small line. The lines that decide whether a firm got bigger are the **wrap fee** and the **FX spread**.

If the client converts tugrik at a bank window, the bank books the spread and the broker books a ticket. A broker inside a bank group and a broker that sends clients to a bank are in different businesses. An independent firm with no written split on FX is doing the work and giving away the P&L.

## Japan already did this

On 1 January 2024, Japan made its NISA tax wrapper permanent. A household can put ¥1.2 million a year into a monthly plan, up to a lifetime cap of ¥18 million. The foreign index sits inside a Japanese fund. The client buys the Japanese unit.

- 2023, under the old wrapper: **¥5.2 tn** of purchases
- 2024, all NISA purchases: **¥17.4 tn**, of which ¥11.4 tn into investment trusts

About ¥5.0 trillion of 2024 buying was standing monthly debits into a short list of funds, mostly Japanese funds holding a world index or the S&P 500. And households still held ¥1,134 trillion in cash and deposits. **The deposit pile stayed. A slice moved.**

Yen deposits paid a fraction of a percent. Ours pay 11.7%, so the same slice won't move on Tokyo's timetable. What's worth copying is the order: a permanent wrapper, a monthly debit, and a local fund with the foreign index inside.

## A year of work that would change the size of a firm

1. **One household shelf, with the deposit on the same page.** Roughly 80% short USD government exposure, 20% a broad equity index, in a non-US fund shell. Say plainly, in Mongolian, that in a calm year this can earn less than a deposit. The clients who stay are the ones who wanted the insurance.
2. **A debit, not a ticket.** A standing monthly amount sized for a household, or don't call it retail. Track funded accounts, average debit, and revenue after FX, custody and platform costs.
3. **Tax and death on the same page as the yield.** One paragraph, reviewed by Mongolian and US counsel. If you can't write it, the product isn't ready.
4. **FX inside the offer.** Either the firm converts, or a written deal says who keeps the spread.
5. **A separate book for exporters.** Yuan liquidity, gold, and a rule for how much dollar cash a company holds against tugrik costs. Not the household plan with a new cover.
6. **Three things that won't make you bigger:** another execution badge, a pitch to the social-insurance fund, and a promise to beat 11.7%.
